Warren Buffett's 'Buy and Hold Forever' Is Not Blind Dogma: When Value Investors Should Sell and Rebalance

The Hidden Prerequisites of Long-Term Compounding and the Discipline of Dynamic Rebalancing

In the daily grind of financial markets, investors constantly battle their own emotions. Every morning when the opening bell rings, they obsessively stare at flickering green and red quotes. If a stock drops just 3%, their mood plummets to rock bottom, triggering spirals of self-doubt. Yet when prices surge, euphoric excitement takes over, tempting them to chase rallies and sink every last dollar of savings. Even worse, after blindly chasing hot trends and getting trapped in deep drawdowns, many quickly comfort themselves with an excuse: “I'm a value investor; I'm holding for the long haul.”

In reality, “buy and hold” has become one of the most misunderstood and abused concepts in the entire investing world. When retail investors face steep losses, they often treat long-term holding as a psychological escape from reality. Without objective evaluation standards, stubborn holding only guarantees permanent capital destruction.

To dismantle the myth that traps so many investors, we must re-examine the core logic beneath Warren Buffett's investment philosophy and build a rational, objective system for dynamic review and portfolio adjustment.

Re-examining Warren Buffett's Famous Quote

In his 1988 Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letter, Warren Buffett wrote a legendary sentence quoted across decades: “When we own portions of outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, our favorite holding period is forever.”

Warren Buffett's 1988 Shareholder Letter:

“When we own portions of outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, our favorite holding period is forever.”

Countless investors worldwide treat this quote as gospel. Yet the vast majority remember only the second half—“holding period is forever”—while completely overlooking the strict, demanding prerequisite in the first half. When what you own is not an elite enterprise backed by a durable moat and high-integrity management, “holding forever” turns into a catastrophe of capital annihilation.

The psychological defense mechanism behind bag-holding

When markets are running hot, everyday investors rarely resist the temptation. Seeing trending tickers discussed all over social media and stock prices skyrocketing day after day, they load up at peak valuations driven by FOMO (Fear of Missing Out). Soon after, the market cycle turns, and shares plunge 50% or even 70%. At that moment of intense pain, the human brain kicks its innate psychological defense mechanisms into gear.

Behavioral finance calls this phenomenon the “Disposition Effect” and the “Sunk Cost Fallacy.” Investors cannot bear the emotional blow of acknowledging a mistake by selling, so they instantly rebrand themselves as steadfast long-term compounders.

This mindset is purely an ostrich strategy that avoids reality. Mr. Market does not care about your purchase price. Whether you have owned a stock for five days or five years, its future performance depends exclusively on the company's real ability to generate future free cash flow. Using long-term holding to rationalize a speculative mistake has nothing to do with genuine value investing.

Long-term holding is a natural byproduct, not a dogma to obey blindly

The businesses Buffett considers worthy of “holding forever” are extraordinarily rare anomalies in the corporate world. Take Coca-Cola (KO), for instance. This company boasts irreplaceable global brand pricing power, minimal capital expenditure needs, an endless torrent of free cash flow, and a laser-sharp focus on its core business. When you are fortunate enough to acquire a stake in such an elite economic castle at a fair price, holding it through the decades allows the miracle of compounding to unfold without unnecessary brokerage fees and capital gains taxes.

However, long-term holding is the natural result of enduring business success; it must never devolve into an unyielding dogma.

As the famous 20th-century economist John Maynard Keynes remarked: “When the facts change, I change my mind. What do you do, sir?” The reason mature value investors survive and thrive over decades is their rational agility to revise their hypotheses whenever objective facts change, rather than clinging to dogma and engaging in self-deception.

Dissecting Warren Buffett's Selling Decisions

Buffett does not lock stocks away in a safe and ignore them forever. If you examine Berkshire Hathaway's investment track record, you will discover that Buffett is remarkably decisive when it is time to sell.

When the foundational thesis dissolves, when valuations inflate into irrational bubbles, or when external structural risks escalate, Buffett never hesitates to pull the trigger.

Berkshire's Classic Selling and Trimming Case Studies
1. Wells Fargo (WFC): The Collapse of Management Integrity

Wells Fargo was once Buffett's favorite financial holding. Berkshire held the bank for over three decades, standing by it through multiple financial crises. Buffett repeatedly praised its conservative lending culture and low-cost deposit moat.

In 2016, however, Wells Fargo was rocked by the nationwide “fake accounts” scandal. Branch employees, pressured by unrealistic cross-selling quotas from headquarters, secretly opened millions of unauthorized credit cards and bank accounts for unsuspecting customers, levying hidden fees. Subsequent investigations exposed that senior executives had known about the malpractice for years, intentionally covering it up while retaliating against internal whistleblowers.

For a banking institution that exists solely on the trust of depositors and regulators, moral bankruptcy destroys the core goodwill moat. Buffett recognized that this was not a temporary operational hiccup, but an irreversible structural crack in governance. Berkshire promptly initiated a multi-quarter exit, eventually liquidating its entire position after thirty years of ownership. This case exemplifies a golden rule: when management integrity shatters, value investors must enforce zero tolerance.

2. Delta Air Lines (DAL) and the Carriers: Decisive Loss-Cutting on Broken Business Models

In early 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic erupted without warning. Nations slammed borders shut, and global corporate and leisure travel evaporated overnight. At the time, Berkshire held over 10% stakes across the four major U.S. carriers—Delta (DAL), Southwest (LUV), United (UAL), and American (AAL)—representing billions of dollars in invested capital.

Airlines suffer from heavy capital assets, massive fixed operating costs, and enormous aircraft lease obligations. Faced with collapsed passenger volumes, airlines were incinerating tens of millions of dollars in cash every single day. More critically, Buffett anticipated that video conferencing tools might permanently impair lucrative corporate business travel, while carriers were forced to take government bailout loans with severe dilution and restrictions, heavily eroding equity value.

At the May 2020 Berkshire annual meeting, Buffett openly admitted his mistake to shareholders and announced that Berkshire had sold its entire airline portfolio within weeks, absorbing billions in realized losses. Buffett calmly stated: “The world has changed for the airlines... We do not fund businesses where we think they're going to chew up money in the future.” When an industry's structural business model suffers irreparable destruction, acknowledging errors swiftly and walking away is the bravest way to protect your capital.

3. TSMC (TSM): Swift Tactical Reallocation Under Changing Geopolitical Boundaries

In Q3 2022, Berkshire grabbed global headlines by deploying over $4.1 billion into Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSM), the unrivaled king of advanced semiconductor foundries. Yet just one quarter later, Berkshire stunned the market by slashing 86% of its stake in Q4 2022, before liquidating the remaining shares in Q1 2023.

At the subsequent shareholder meeting, Buffett offered glowing praise for TSMC: “It is one of the best-managed companies and most important companies in the world. There’s no one in the chip industry that’s in their league.” But he immediately pivoted to the core issue: geopolitical risk.

TSMC is headquartered across the sensitive Taiwan Strait. If regional tensions flare, the downside risk transcends ordinary commercial competition. Although TSMC's earnings power and technological moat remain peerless, the macroeconomic and geopolitical boundary conditions exceeded Berkshire’s strict threshold for capital safety. Buffett redeployed that capital into Japan's five major trading houses, which met his criteria for peace of mind. This case underscores that sudden shifts in macro external constraints justify dynamic capital reallocation.

4. Apple (AAPL): Trimming at High Valuations to Rebalance Concentration Risk

Since Buffett built his stake in 2016, Apple (AAPL) has proven to be one of the most profitable investments in Berkshire's storied history. Propelled by its sticky iOS ecosystem, unrivaled user loyalty, and aggressive corporate share buybacks, Apple shares rallied year after year. By late 2023, Apple alone ballooned to over 50% of Berkshire's massive public equity portfolio.

In the first half of 2024, SEC filings revealed that Buffett trimmed roughly half of Berkshire's Apple holdings, converting enormous unrealized capital gains into short-term Treasury bills and liquid cash.

This move did not mean Buffett had soured on Apple’s brand power. Analyzing the rationale reveals two main drivers: first, allowing a single asset to exceed 50% of total equities concentrated unacceptable non-systemic risk; second, Apple’s P/E multiple had climbed past 30x into historical highs, fully pricing in future growth. Factoring in potential future increases in U.S. capital gains taxes, Buffett chose to harvest gains at the peak. This demonstrates how disciplined portfolio dynamic rebalancing shields you against extreme concentration.

The Three Triggers for Adjusting a Position

A value investor’s playbook never relies on short-term chart patterns or technical indicators. Buying and selling stocks should be a calculated, rational decision anchored purely in business fundamentals and capital allocation.

Trigger 1: Thesis broken

Whenever you purchase a stock, you should write down a clear “Investment Thesis Checklist.” Throughout your holding period, you must periodically test whether those assumptions remain intact.

  • What specific moat allows this company to generate superior profits?
  • Exclusive patents, switching costs, network effects, or massive economies of scale?

Investors must objectively differentiate between “short-term operational headwinds” and “structural decay.”

  • Short-Term Operational Headwinds (No need to panic): Temporary commodity spikes, cyclical macro downturns, quarterly currency fluctuations, or short-term supply chain bottlenecks. These are normal, unavoidable hiccups in business. As long as core competitive advantages remain intact, market panic merely offers a bargain discount.
  • Structural Decay (Exit decisively): Disruptive technology rendering products obsolete (such as generative AI reshaping enterprise SaaS software models), fierce cross-industry entrants stripping away pricing power, customers permanently abandoning the ecosystem, or systemic executive fraud and governance rot. This is a classic value trap.

Once the underlying commercial thesis that justified your capital allocation is shattered by objective reality, hoping for the stock to “bounce back” is dangerous delusion. The only rational response is to acknowledge the mistake and exit cleanly.

Trigger 2: Extreme valuation squeezing out margin of safety

Benjamin Graham’s “Mr. Market” suffers from intense bipolar mood swings. When he is gripped by manic optimism, he quotes wildly irrational acquisition prices.

During peak speculative bubbles, euphoric sentiment catapults popular companies to dizzying heights. Price-to-earnings multiples vault past 80x or 100x, while Free Cash Flow (FCF) yields compress below 1%—far below risk-free U.S. Treasury yields. Such pricing embeds perfectionist assumptions, demanding flawless, hyper-speed earnings growth for a decade without a single operational stumble.

Holding a position at these levels exposes you to an asymmetric, unfavorable risk-reward profile. You are risking a potential 50% drawdown just to chase the final scraps of speculative froth.

The heart of value investing is the “margin of safety.” When euphoric markets squeeze that safety buffer down to zero, trimming your stake or harvesting your gains is an act of rational capital protection.

Trigger 3: Opportunity cost and superior capital allocation

Charlie Munger frequently stressed that the cornerstone of intelligent thinking is “opportunity cost.”

Investors should view themselves as the “Chief Capital Allocation Officer of their personal holding company.” Your primary duty is ensuring that every dollar of precious capital operates at maximum productive efficiency, free from emotional attachment to any single company.

Suppose Asset A in your portfolio is operating steadily, but based on current valuations, its expected forward annualized return is only 5% to 6%. Meanwhile, sudden macroeconomic panic drives Asset B—a premier enterprise with a fortress balance sheet and an equally deep moat—down to bargain valuations, offering a 15% expected annual return with a 30% margin of safety discount.

In that scenario, selling Asset A to purchase Asset B is an act of rational capital efficiency optimization, not restless trading. Superior investors constantly pit their current holdings against the market's best alternative opportunities.

Position Sizing and the Sleep-at-Night Threshold

Even with sharp business acumen, investors still wash out during market downturns. The root cause is rarely stock selection—it is runaway position sizing and emotional burnout.

Even the world's most resilient enterprises experience 30% to 50% price corrections along their journey. If you lack defensive position management, Mr. Market’s violent turbulence will punch straight through your emotional armor.

The double-edged sword of concentration

Buffett and Munger have long championed concentrated investing within one's circle of competence, advocating heavy bets on a handful of elite businesses. However, high concentration is a double-edged sword.

When a winner surges over several years, it can naturally expand to 40% or more of your net worth. At that threshold, idiosyncratic risk skyrockets; any unforeseen industry disruption or company-specific setback can inflict permanent damage on your life savings.

The ultimate benchmark for testing position size is the “Sleep-at-Night Test.”

As your head hits the pillow each night, ask yourself: “If this stock opens down 10% tomorrow morning, will my heart race and my workday derail?” If the answer is yes, the position has breached your psychological threshold. Regardless of how enticing the valuation may appear, trimming exposure is essential to safeguard your emotional discipline.

The defensive power of dynamic rebalancing

Many investors mistakenly view portfolio rebalancing as a form of short-term market timing. In reality, it is a calm, disciplined defense mechanism.

When an asset becomes overvalued and balloons into an outsized proportion of your portfolio, systematically taking partial profits and redeploying cash into safe short-term Treasuries, dry powder, or neglected bargains restores balance.

Dynamic rebalancing delivers dual benefits: first, it forces you to lock in gains during euphoric rallies, smoothing portfolio volatility; second, it guarantees you hold ample dry powder when black swan storms strike, allowing you to go on the offensive while others panic.

Never fall in love with your stocks

Wall Street offers a timeless warning: “Never fall in love with a stock, because it doesn't know you own it.” While this sounds contrary to Buffett’s philosophy of owning businesses like a partnership, emotional detachment toward liquid assets is vital.

If a stock has made you a fortune in the past, you subconsciously develop affection for the ticker. Even if management later loses discipline or squanders capital, you find yourself making excuses for them.

Psychologists call this “Commitment and Consistency Bias.” Investors must learn to separate personal emotion from cold, objective capital allocation.

How Often Should You Review Your Portfolio?

If value investing requires dynamic adjustments, does that mean you should sit glued to financial screens every day? Absolutely not.

Excessive monitoring bombards the brain with toxic noise, triggering impulsive overtrading. Conversely, complete neglect leaves you unprepared when structural crises strike. Mature value investors establish a clear, sustainable rhythm that fits seamlessly into daily life.

Investor Portfolio Review Rhythm
1. Daily Routine: Mindset Shield

Break the daily ticker-watching habit → Filter out Mr. Market's emotional noise → Focus on career, family, and real life.

2. Quarterly Checkup: 15-Minute 10-Q Review

Moat intact? → Cash flow healthy? → Initial thesis valid? → Valuation reasonable? (If all 4 criteria are healthy, do absolutely nothing).

3. Annual Deep Audit: 10-K & Shareholder Letters

Audit management promises → Review ROE & capital allocation efficiency → Execute disciplined dynamic rebalancing.

4. Special Exceptions: Major Structural Black Swans

Systemic accounting fraud / Ruinous regulatory bans / Destructive mega-mergers → Trigger immediate fundamental re-evaluation.

Quit daily ticker-watching and tune out Mr. Market

Excessive real-time price monitoring warps your investing psychology. Second-by-second price fluctuations reflect Mr. Market’s mood swings, completely divorced from everyday business operations. A thriving coffee shop does not change in intrinsic value between 10:00 AM and 2:00 PM on a Tuesday.

Watching stock tickers burns cognitive energy, magnifies the fear of paper drawdowns, and ultimately lures you into rash decisions.

Once a quarter: A 15-minute 10-Q health check

The only time investors need to pay focused attention is during quarterly earnings releases and conference calls. This does not take days—enjoying a single cup of coffee while reviewing headline fundamentals is plenty, especially with modern AI analytical tools.

If the following four criteria check out, the best action to take is: do nothing at all.

  1. Is the economic moat intact? Are core gross margins and operating margins steady? Does the company maintain pricing power? Are customer churn rates normal?
  2. Is financial health robust? Is operating cash flow turning reliably into positive free cash flow (FCF)? Are short-term debt maturities well covered?
  3. Does the initial investment thesis hold true? Are the core business drivers that led to your initial purchase still progressing on track?
  4. Has valuation entered bubble territory? Has the recent stock rally disconnected from multi-year earnings fundamentals?

Once a year: Deep annual audit (10-K and shareholder letters)

Every spring when companies publish annual 10-K reports and shareholder letters, conduct a thorough annual audit.

  1. Verify Management Integrity: Open the shareholder letter from three or five years ago. Compare management’s stated targets against actual multi-year execution. Have promises been kept, or are excuses piling up? Transparent management is a priceless intangible asset.
  2. Evaluate Capital Allocation Efficiency: Examine how management deployed retained earnings into expansion, acquisitions, or buybacks. Did those capital expenditures generate above-average returns, such as sustained ROE above 15%?
  3. Recalculate Intrinsic Value and Rebalance: Based on the full year’s free cash flow, update your intrinsic value estimates and adjust portfolio concentration through dynamic rebalancing where appropriate.

Special exceptions: Response protocol for structural crises

Outside of quarterly and annual cadences, investors only need to intervene when a company suffers an acute structural crisis.

Examples include criminal fraud charges against top executives, an adverse court ruling invalidating a cornerstone patent, a sudden government ban shutting down an entire product line, or an announcement of an overpriced, debt-fueled empire-building merger. When confronted by a foundational earthquake threatening business solvency, acting promptly to cut losses serves as your final capital firewall.

Takeaways from Mr.Cafe

When you trace this investment mindset to its roots, you realize that the true elegance of value investing lies in the serenity and freedom gained from grasping business reality. Long-term holding is the vehicle for capturing business compounding, but it must never become an unthinking religious dogma.

The goal of investing is not to prove you never make mistakes. The goal is to ensure that every dollar of capital remains deployed where the probability of success is highest, where moats are deepest, and where valuations are safest. That is the timeless lesson Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger offer us:

  1. Unshakeable Patience: When premier businesses face temporary cyclical headwinds or irrational market selloffs, adopt an owner's mindset and comfortably weather the storm, completely ignoring everyday price noise.
  2. Cold Rationality: When objective reality proves that your initial investment thesis is shattered, management integrity has collapsed, or valuations have disconnected from reality, let go of emotional attachment, accept the mistake, and exit decisively.
